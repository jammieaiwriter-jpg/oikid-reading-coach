A Morning on the Cherry Blossom Trail

對話（多聲線）
語速：

Ivy: Wow, look at all these cherry blossoms! The whole trail is pink today.

Paul: I know! My mom said we should come early before it gets crowded.

Ivy: Good idea. Are the flowers fully open yet?

Paul: Almost. My uncle checked yesterday, and he said they are about eighty percent open.

Ivy: Perfect timing, then. Let's walk to the old bridge first.

Paul: Sure. I heard the petals fall like light pink snow when the wind blows.

Ivy: That sounds beautiful. Do you want to take some photos there?

Paul: Yes, but let's also just enjoy it without a phone for a while.

Ivy: I like that idea. We can take photos on the way back.

Paul: This tree looks the oldest. Its trunk is thick and a little bent.

Ivy: Maybe it has been here for over a hundred years.

Paul: That is amazing to think about. Let's sit under it for a minute.

Ivy: Good idea. I never want to leave this trail.

Paul: Me neither. Let's come back next weekend if the flowers are still here.

A Night Walk to See Fireflies

短文
語速：

Every May, a small forest near my grandmother's house turns into a firefly forest. My cousin Owen and I visit every year, but this year we finally go there together after dark.

We meet at the trail entrance at seven thirty. A volunteer guide gives each visitor a red flashlight, because a bright white light can scare the fireflies away and stop them from glowing.

The trail is narrow and a little wet, so we walk slowly and follow the guide in a single line. Nobody talks loudly, and the forest is quiet except for a small stream.

Then, near a dark patch of bushes, we see the first light. It blinks on and off like a tiny yellow star, and a few seconds later, dozens more lights appear all around us.

The guide tells us that fireflies use their light to find a partner. Male fireflies fly and blink, and female fireflies wait on leaves and blink back if they like the pattern.

Owen counts more than fifty lights before we leave. On the walk back, the firefly forest feels like a different, quieter world, and I already want to come back again next May.

Why Mangroves Love the Tide

短文
語速：

Along parts of Taiwan's coast, a special kind of forest grows where the land meets the sea. These plants are called mangroves, and they can live in salty water that most trees can not live in.

At high tide, seawater rises and covers most of the mangrove roots. From a boat or a bridge, the trees can look like they are sitting on top of the water with only their leaves showing.

Mangrove roots grow in a special shape. Many of them curve up out of the mud like small arches and then curve back down again. These special roots help the tree breathe even when the mud is very wet.

The mud around mangrove roots is full of life. Crabs dig small holes, and fish and shrimp hide between the roots when the tide is high. Birds often stand close by and wait for a meal.

When the tide moves back out, the roots and mud appear again, and visitors can walk near the roots on paths made of wood. Guides say it is good to visit both at high tide and low tide to see the whole picture.

Mangrove forests also protect the coast. Their thick roots slow down waves and hold the mud in place, so the land near the coast does not wash away as quickly during storms.

A Letter About Autumn Leaf-Sweeping Day

信件
語速：

Dear neighbors,

Fall is here, and the leaves on our street are turning yellow, orange, and brown. Every year around this time, our small park looks full of color, but the ground gets covered with leaves that fall from the trees.

I am writing to invite everyone to our Autumn Leaf-Sweeping Day this Saturday morning. We will meet at the park entrance at nine o'clock and sweep the leaves together as a group.

Please wear old clothes and bring a rake if you have one at home. We will also have some bags for collecting the leaves, so you don't need to bring your own trash bags.

After we finish sweeping, we will sit together in the garden and share some warm tea and snacks. It is a nice way to say thank you to everyone who comes to help.

My children always enjoy this day. They like to jump into the piles of leaves before we put them into bags, and they always end up laughing the whole morning.

If the weather is too cold or rainy on Saturday, we will move the event to the next weekend instead. I will send another message if that happens.

I hope to see many of you there. Working together always makes our little street feel like one big team, and it only takes one morning each fall.

Thank you for reading, and I look forward to seeing you and your family this Saturday!

Sincerely, Your neighbor

The Covered Walkways of the Rainy Season

短文
語速：

In Taiwan, many streets have a special kind of covered walkway in front of the shops. People call this space a covered walkway, and it runs along almost every block in busy cities and small towns.

This design is especially helpful during the rainy season in early summer, when it can rain for many days without stopping. Under the walkway, people can walk from shop to shop and stay dry the whole time.

The walkway also gives shade on sunny days. In summer, the sun can be very strong, so walking under the roof keeps people cooler and more comfortable.

Many people also use the space for other things. Shop owners sometimes put chairs or small tables there, and some families park their bikes along the wall instead of leaving them out in the open.

During the rainy season, the ground under the walkway can still get a little wet from wind and rain, so people often choose shoes that dry quickly and keep their feet more comfortable.

This kind of walkway has been part of local city life for a long time. Old buildings and new buildings both have this same style, so the streets look connected from block to block.

For visitors from other places, this covered walkway can feel new and interesting. Many say it is one of the most useful designs they have ever seen in a city.

Next time it rains, try walking under a covered walkway instead of using an umbrella. You may find that your feet stay dry, and the walk feels much more comfortable than you expected.

The Market Before a Typhoon

短文
語速：

Every summer, when the news says a big typhoon is coming, people in our town rush to the market before the storm arrives. My mother and I go early in the morning to buy food.

The market is already full of people. Everyone wants to buy vegetables, eggs, and instant noodles before the shops close. My mother says we need enough food for three or four days.

At one shop, the shopkeeper tells us the price of cabbage has gone up because farmers could not send more from the south. My mother decides to buy a little less this time.

While we are picking fruit, I hear two women talking about the wind and rain in the news. One woman says, I hope the typhoon turns and goes another way this time.

My mother buys bread, water, and a battery for our flashlight. She says these things are always useful during a typhoon, even if the storm is not very strong.

By noon, most shops start to close early. The shopkeepers want to go home and get their own families ready before the wind gets too strong.

On our way home, the sky turns dark, and the wind feels cooler than before. My mother says, Let's hurry, I don't want to get wet before we reach home.

That night, the wind and rain are loud outside, but our home is warm and safe. I think about the busy market that morning, and I am glad we went early.

The Beach Cleanup Day

短文
語速：

Last Saturday, my class went to the beach for a special event. Our teacher has organized this beach cleanup every year since she started teaching at our school. This year, more students joined than ever before.

When we arrived, the beach was covered with plastic bottles, old bags, and small pieces of trash.

Our teacher gave each of us a pair of gloves and a big bag. She told us to work in pairs and pick up as much trash as we could.

I worked with my friend Leo. We walked along the shore and picked up bottle caps, straws, and pieces of rope. Some trash was hidden under the sand, so we had to dig a little. After two hours, our bags were almost full.

At noon, the teacher counted all the bags. Our class had collected more than one hundred bags of trash! That was the biggest number the school has ever collected. Everyone clapped and cheered.

Before we left, a worker from the city thanked us. She said the beach looked much cleaner than before we came. She also said our class was the most helpful group she had seen all year.

On the bus home, I felt tired but happy. I have never worked so hard for something outside of school. I hope our class can do this cleanup again next year, because a clean beach makes everyone happy.

Foot Baths at the Hot Spring Park

短文
語速：

Have you ever visited a hot spring park? In many mountain towns, people can enjoy a free foot bath and skip the full hot spring bath. A foot bath is smaller and more simple, but it is still very relaxing.

The water in a foot bath comes from natural hot springs under the ground. It is usually hotter than regular water, so visitors only put their feet in, not their whole body.

Most foot baths are made with stone seats around a long pool.

Doctors say a foot bath is good for your health. When your feet are warm, it is good for the blood in your whole body too. Some people say a foot bath after a long walk feels more relaxing than resting on a chair.

More people visit foot bath parks on weekends than on weekdays. Families like to sit together, talk, and rest their feet at the same time.

Children usually enjoy it the most, because they can move their feet in the warm water and laugh together.

Many hot spring towns have made more than one foot bath park in the last few years. Some parks have added music and lights to make the place more interesting at night.

Local shops near the parks also do more business, because visitors often stop for tea or snacks after a foot bath.

If you visit a hot spring town, try a foot bath. It costs little or nothing, and it might become the most relaxing part of your trip.

Harvest Day in the Rice Field

對話（多聲線）
語速：

Grandpa: Today is harvest day. The rice has turned golden, and it is ready to cut.

Mia: I have never helped with a harvest before. What should I do first?

Grandpa: First, put on these boots. The field is muddy after the rain.

Mia: Okay. Should I use this big machine, or do people still cut rice by hand?

Grandpa: Most farmers use a machine now. It is faster to use a machine than to cut by hand, but some small fields are still cut by hand.

Mia: This field looks smaller than the one next door.

Grandpa: Yes, ours is one of the smallest fields in the village, but people say the rice here tastes very good.

Mia: Really? Why is that?

Grandpa: People have planted rice in this soil for over a hundred years. It is richer than newer fields.

Mia: That is amazing. Has this field always belonged to our family?

Grandpa: Yes. This field has always belonged to our family, and we have never let it go.

Mia: I am glad we still have it. Can I help drive the machine?

Grandpa: Not yet, it is too dangerous for beginners. But you can help carry the bags of rice to the truck.

Mia: Okay! I will try hard. This is more exciting than I thought it would be.

Why Bamboo Forests Feel So Cool and Quiet

短文
語速：

Many mountain parks in Taiwan have walking paths that pass through tall bamboo forests. Visitors often notice that the air under the bamboo feels cooler and quieter than the open path outside.

One reason is shade. Bamboo grows very tall and close together, and its narrow leaves block most of the direct sun. This shade keeps the ground and the air below much cooler, even on a hot afternoon.

Sound also changes inside a bamboo forest. The thick rows of bamboo stems act like a natural wall, so noise from roads or crowds outside becomes quiet and far away. Only the sound of wind that moves through the leaves stays clear.

That quiet sound has a special meaning in local culture. People often compare the whisper of wind through bamboo leaves to a gentle, calm voice. Many visitors say a walk on a bamboo path helps them rest and think well.

Bamboo also grows in a special way. It does not grow thicker every year like a tree. It sends out new shoots from roots under the ground, and in just a few weeks, a young shoot can grow into a tall, strong stem.

This fast growing plant explains why bamboo forests can stay thick and green all year. Even after workers cut down some old stems for building or furniture, new shoots soon rise up and take their place.

Because bamboo paths stay cool, quiet, and green through every season, they are one of the most popular spots for visitors to rest during a long mountain walk.

A Letter About My Camping Trip

信件
語速：

Dear Alex,

I want to tell you about my camping trip last weekend! My family drove up into the mountains on Friday afternoon and set up our tent before it got dark.

After dinner, we stayed near a small campfire and cooked marshmallows over the warm fire. The night air was cool, so everyone put on a jacket and stayed close to the fire.

Later, we turned off our flashlights and looked up at the sky. Without the city lights, we could see hundreds of stars, and my little brother even saw a shooting star fly across the sky.

The campsite was full of quiet night sounds. We could hear bugs singing in the grass and an owl calling somewhere in the trees. At first the sounds were a little strange, but soon they were calm and peaceful.

We went to bed inside our tent that night, and I got up early the next morning to the sound of birds. The air smelled fresh, and the mountains looked beautiful in the morning light.

I hope you can come camping with my family next time. I think you would really enjoy watching the stars and listening to the sounds of the forest at night.

Your friend,

Eric

A Morning in the Tea Garden

短文
語速：

Every summer, Lily visits her grandfather's tea garden in the mountains. The garden sits on a green hillside, and rows of tea plants stretch from the bottom of the hill to the top.

This morning, Lily wakes up before the sun rises. Her grandfather says the best tea leaves are picked early, while the leaves are still cool and fresh from the night air.

Lily puts on a wide hat and carries a small basket on her back. She follows her grandfather between the rows of tea plants, learning how to pick only the top two leaves and one bud.

At first, Lily picks too slowly, and her basket stays almost empty. Her grandfather laughs kindly and shows her how to move her fingers quickly without hurting the plant.

By noon, the sun is high, and Lily's basket is finally full of fresh green leaves. Her back is tired, but she feels proud of the work she has done.

After lunch, Lily watches the workers spread the leaves on large flat trays to dry in the warm air. Her grandfather explains that drying the leaves the right way makes the tea taste better.

In the evening, the family sits together and drinks a cup of tea made from leaves picked that very morning. The tea tastes light and a little sweet, and everyone agrees it is the best cup of the whole year.

Before Lily goes to bed, she tells her grandfather that she wants to help pick tea again next summer. He smiles and says the garden will always be waiting for her.
