What Happens at a School Charity Bazaar

短文
語速：

Many schools in Taiwan hold a charity bazaar once a year, usually near the end of the school term.

At a charity bazaar, each class sets up a small stall and sells food, drinks, or things they made to other students and parents.

Students plan their stall together weeks before the event, and they decide what to sell and how to decorate their table.

Some classes sell snacks like popcorn or cold drinks, while other classes sell simple games for a small ticket price.

The money that a class earns from the bazaar does not belong to the students themselves.

Instead, the school usually collects all the money and donates it to a hospital for children or another charity group.

Teachers often remind students that the goal of the bazaar is not to make the most money, but to help people in need.

Parents and neighbors are welcome to visit the school on this day, and the whole campus feels like a small festival.

By the end of the afternoon, most classes have nothing left to sell, and students count their coins together before handing them to a teacher.

For many students, the charity bazaar is one of the most exciting and important days of the whole school year.

The Last Leg of the Relay

短文
語速：

Every fall, Ren-Hao's school has a sports day. The last event is always a relay race between all the classes.

This year, Ren-Hao's class is in first place. They are ahead by only a few seconds. Ren-Hao will run the last part of the race.

He waits at the starting line. His hands feel cold. He watches his teammate run closer with the baton.

The baton reaches his hand. Ren-Hao holds it tightly and starts running as fast as he can.

A runner from another class is right beside him. For a moment, Ren-Hao is not sure who will win.

He remembers what his coach told him. Keep your eyes on the finish line. Do not look at the other runners.

Ren-Hao runs harder in the last few steps. His classmates yell loudly from the crowd.

He crosses the finish line first, just a little ahead of the other runner. His whole class jumps up and cheers.

After the race, Ren-Hao's legs feel weak. But he smiles, because his team worked so hard to win.

The teacher gives the class a big trophy. Everyone takes a photo together before they go back to their seats.

A Letter About the Temple Parade

信件
語速：

Dear Hao-Ting,

I hope you are well. Last weekend, our town was full of noise because of a big temple parade, and I want to tell you all about it.

The parade started early in the morning near the old temple by the river. Loud drums and firecrackers filled the street, and I could feel the sound everywhere around me.

Groups of people had colorful costumes and walked together like a small army. Some of them had tall masks and walked with slow, heavy steps, while others jumped and turned quickly to the beat of the drums.

My grandfather said that these groups are the guards of the temple, and each one practices together for months before every parade. He said the tradition is very old, and every group of neighbors is proud of their team.

We followed the parade through several streets, and neighbors came out of their houses to watch and cheer. My little brother covered his ears because of the loud firecrackers, but he still had a big smile the whole time.

At the end of the parade, everyone gathered near the temple for a small ceremony, and the leader gave a short speech about keeping the tradition alive for many more years.

I really hope you can visit next year and see this parade with me. It is something you will never forget.

Love,

Pei-Chen

Lost in the Lantern Festival Crowd

短文
語速：

Every February, my family visits the Lantern Festival in our town. This year, the streets were so full of people that we could not move fast between the food stands and the paper lanterns.

My little cousin wanted to see the big dragon lantern near the river, so we walked slowly through the crowd. Colorful lights were hanging above us, and music played from every direction.

While my father was buying tea eggs, I turned around to show my cousin a rabbit lantern. When I looked back a minute later, I did not see my father there.

My heart started to beat fast. Thousands of people were around me, and every jacket looked the same in the dark. I took my cousin by the hand and wanted to stay calm.

Then I remembered a large sign near the main gate. It said, "Lost? Meet here." We walked back through the crowd toward that sign, checking every face on the way.

After ten long minutes, I saw my father standing under the sign. Both his arms were high in the air, and he looked more worried than I did.

We made a simple rule for next year: if anyone in the group is lost, everyone should walk straight back to the sign near the gate.

It was wonderful to reunite as one group again. What a night! I will always remember how one small sign turned a scary moment into a happy ending.

Why Cities Love New Year's Eve Fireworks

短文
語速：

Every December 31, millions of people around the world stand outside in the cold to watch a big firework show light up the sky. This tradition grows more popular every single year.

Many big cities now plan their New Year's Eve show for almost the whole year before it happens. A team of workers sets up rocket after rocket along a tall building or a bridge.

Each firework lights up the sky with music playing at the same time. Some shows use computers to control the second every rocket fires, so the colors match the beat of the song.

Cities also choose the color of each firework for a reason. Bright gold often stands for good luck, while red is a common color in many Asian countries because people think it is a lucky color.

Safety is one of the most important jobs for the team. Workers check the weather, the wind, and the distance between the crowd and the firework show before it can start.

After the countdown reaches zero, the sky fills with light for several minutes. People often hug, take photos, or stand still and watch, and the cold does not seem to matter at all.

For many families, watching the firework show together has become as important as the show itself. It is a moment that marks the end of one year and the start of another.

What a sight it is when the whole sky turns into color at once! It is no surprise this simple tradition brings so many strangers together every December 31.

A Letter from Backstage

信件
語速：

Dear Mia,

I want to tell you about my day at the speech contest. I am waiting backstage right now, so my hands still feel a little cold.

Before my turn, I practice my speech many times at home every day this week. Although I still feel nervous, I try to stay calm. My teacher always says the same thing: if you forget one line, do not stop, just keep speaking.

While I stand backstage, I can hear the microphone on the stage. Every voice on the stage sounds loud and clear. I listen to a girl before me, and I cannot believe how well she speaks.

As soon as my name is called, I take a deep breath and walk to the stage. My legs feel weak, but I look at the judges and begin my speech.

I forget one small line, but I do what my teacher says. I do not stop. I turn to the next line, and no one seems to notice.

When I finish, the whole room begins to cheer and clap. My principal gives me a thumbs up from the back row, and I feel so proud.

Until the judges finish, no one knows who will win a medal. But I already feel like a winner, because I do not give up.

I will call you tonight and tell you everything.

Love,

Wei

Why Every Summer Camp Has a Campfire

短文
語速：

A campfire is one of the most popular activities at many summer camps. Every evening, camp leaders set up a circle of chairs near a safe, open area, far away from the trees and tents.

Before campers sit down, an adult always checks the wind and the ground. If the wind is too strong, the camp may not light a fire that night, because a small fire could quickly turn into a big problem.

While the fire burns, campers often sing songs, tell stories, or share a funny memory from the day. Music from a guitar can make the whole group feel warm and close, even on a cold night.

Many camps also cook marshmallows near the campfire. Campers hold a marshmallow close to the warm fire until it turns soft and a little brown, and then they share it with a friend.

Camp leaders teach a few important safety rules before the campfire begins. Campers must never leave a fire alone, and they must always keep water or sand near the fire area.

As soon as the songs and stories end, an adult pours water on the fire until it is completely out. Everyone checks the ground one more time before they walk back to their rooms.

Campfires have a long tradition at camps around the world. They are not only about fire and food. They also help a group of strangers become friends, and they give every camper a special memory to keep.

My First Mission at Winter Camp

短文
語速：

Last January, I joined a three-day winter camp in the mountains. When I got off the bus, a leader gave every student a small card and said our first mission was to check in at the front desk.

I picked up my card and walked to the desk. A worker there had a new piece of technology, a small machine. It could read the card in one second and print my team name on it.

While I was waiting in line, I watched other students talk and laugh with their new friends. I was a little nervous, but I was also excited about the days ahead.

My team leader explained the point system. Each student could win points by finishing a mission, helping a friend, or reaching an activity early. The team with the most points would get a special present on the last night.

Before dinner, our team had to pick a team name and a team song. Although some of us were shy, we worked together and picked a name every team member wanted: the Snow Foxes.

That night, a scientist from a university near the camp gave a short talk about the science of ice and speed on the hills. Many students listened and asked questions.

As soon as the talk ended, our leader said we must hurry to our rooms. We had to be ready before nine o'clock, or our team would lose points.

I lay in bed and thought about the busy day. Checking in was only the first mission, but it already seemed like the start of a great winter camp.

How a Water Station Works During a Road Race

短文
語速：

During a road race, water stations appear along the route about every five kilometers. Each station has a team of volunteers who prepare cups of water before the runners arrive.

A water station usually opens about one hour before the first runner is expected. Volunteers set up long tables and fill hundreds of cups, because many runners will pass by at almost the same time.

When runners get close to a station, volunteers hold out cups and call out clearly so runners can find them without slowing down too much. Some runners drink quickly, while others only wet their face and neck.

Besides water, many stations also offer small pieces of banana for extra energy. Bananas are easy to eat quickly, and they help tired runners keep moving toward the finish line.

After each runner passes, volunteers must fill more cups right away, because the next group of runners is never far behind. It is tiring work, but most volunteers say cheering for the runners makes it worth it.

By the end of the race, the ground near a busy station is covered with paper cups. Volunteers pick them up so that the route stays clean and safe for the last runners still on the course.

Getting Our Booth Ready for the Science Fair

短文
語速：

On Friday afternoon, our class carries boxes into the gym, where the science fair will open the next morning. My partner and I are in charge of a booth about how plants grow toward light.

We spread a blue sheet over our table and set up the poster that we made last week. The poster explains our experiment step by step, with photos that show each part of our work.

While we work, a teacher who is helping other groups stops by our booth. She asks a few questions about our results and reminds us to speak clearly during the presentation tomorrow.

Just before we finish, my partner notices that one of our plant pots has tipped over during the move. We carefully lift it back into place and check that nothing inside it has broken.

We also make a short plan for what we will say to the judges, and we practice it together twice. My partner reads the plan out loud while I listen and try to remember every part.

At last, everything looks ready, and small cards for the judges are already set out near the entrance. We take one more look at our table and then head home to rest before the big day.

My First Bank Account

短文
語速：

Last Saturday, my dad took me to a bank near our house to open my first account. I was a little nervous because I had never done anything like this before.

A friendly worker asked me to sit at her desk and gave me a form to fill in. She explained each line slowly so I would not make a mistake.

I filled in my name, my address, and my birthday on the form. My dad signed a second form because I am still under eighteen years old.

The worker asked how much money I wanted to put in first. I gave her the money I had earned from helping neighbors with small jobs.

She counted the money carefully and entered some numbers into her computer. After a few minutes, she printed a small card with my new account number on it.

She explained that I could add more money any time I wanted. She also said that the bank would add a small extra amount of money every year.

My dad said that he had opened his own first account when he was about my age. He said saving a little every month can add up to something big.

Before we left, I asked what would happen if I lost the card. The worker was kind and said the bank could always print me a new one.

On the way home, I made a plan to save part of my allowance every week from now on. I want to watch the number in my account grow little by little.

That night, I explained the whole visit to my little sister. She said she wants to open her own account as soon as she is old enough.

How a Letter Travels Through the Post Office

短文
語速：

Every day, millions of letters and packages pass through post offices around the world. Many people still wait in line to send something important by mail.

First, a customer writes the receiving person's name and address clearly on the front of the envelope. A missing part can make the whole trip take longer.

Next, the customer brings the letter to a worker at the counter. The worker checks the letter's weight and asks how fast the customer wants it to arrive.

The worker then picks the correct stamp by looking at the weight and the distance. A letter with more weight and a longer distance usually needs a higher price.

After the customer pays, the worker puts a special mark on the stamp. This mark shows that people have already used the stamp, so it can not work a second time.

The letter then moves into a large bag with hundreds of other letters. Workers put letters into groups by area so each bag travels on the right truck or plane.

At a center where letters are grouped, machines and workers read every address again. They put letters for nearby towns into one group and letters for faraway towns into another.

Finally, a local mail worker picks up the letters for one area and delivers them door to door. The whole trip can take just one or two days.

Even though many people now send messages online, letters still matter for important papers, gifts, and personal notes from family and friends.
